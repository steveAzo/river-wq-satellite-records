\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Guidance for monitoring agencies}
The results translate into five recommendations for remote river monitoring over satellite IoT:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Send the guaranteed record, not the readings. A day within the USGS excellent-record tolerance needed a median of \needMedConf{} bytes, which is approximately one fiftieth of the raw readings. Evenly spaced readings at the same size violated the tolerance on most days at every loss probability tested (Table~\ref{tab:delivery}).
  \item Protect the record against loss. The design that is optimal on a reliable link guaranteed only \gRelIndPct{} of days at a loss probability of 0.3, and more capacity did not repair it. At that loss probability, repetition and an erasure code both raised the guaranteed share above 80\%. The code is preferable where records are larger, as at the low-cost sites, and repetition is a simpler choice where they are small.
  \item Size the plan for the loss process. With buffering, six messages per day sufficed at \poolSixIndPct{} of sites under independent loss, but bursty loss required 12. A single message per day was insufficient at almost every site.
  \item Choose the plan from a pilot, with a margin for season. A 28-day pilot selected an adequate plan at \sizeSuff{} of sites. Most shortfalls occurred where the pilot fell in a low-need period; therefore, a pilot in the season of greatest variation, or one tier of margin, is advisable.
  \item Do not rank parameters by assumed stability. Temperature consumed the largest share of the guaranteed record (\parShareTemp{}); thus, fixed priority classes that treat it as a baseline would degrade the record where it is most demanding.
\end{enumerate}

Neither satellite contact time nor energy was the binding constraint. An orbit propagation of the Kin\'eis constellation over the Weija site gave approximately \passesWeija{} passes per day above 25\textdegree{} elevation, each lasting a median of \passMinWeija{} minutes. The module draws 500\,mA at 5\,V while transmitting a message for less than one second \citep{kineis2023kim1}; therefore, six messages per day cost at most 15\,J. The constraint was the message quota, which is the quantity this study optimised. Where a terrestrial community LoRaWAN network is available, its fair-use limit of 30 seconds of airtime per day \citep{ttn2026fup} allows approximately \ttnSFtwelve{} bytes per day even at the slowest data rate, more than the largest daily need observed; the scarcity addressed here is therefore specific to satellite plans.

\subsection{Relation to earlier work}
The encoder and the code are established techniques \citep{imai1988,soroush2008,reed1960}, and budgeted compression of sensor history, including allocation of bandwidth towards the node with the largest error, was proposed for multi-hop sensor networks \citep{deligiannakis2007}. The findings of this study qualify that line of work for one-way links: the design that is best when every message arrives was the worst of the guaranteed-record designs once messages could be lost. They also qualify current satellite-tag practice \citep{boehme2009,photopoulou2015}, which fixes the number of points rather than the accuracy; by construction, such a scheme spends capacity on quiet days and cannot guarantee the tolerance on active ones. Freshness-oriented scheduling \citep{yates2021,talli2026} addresses a different objective, the latest value, which a guaranteed record covers as a special case.

\subsection{Limitations}
The results carry the following limitations:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Message plans: the tiers of 1 to 96 messages per day were illustrative; the message size and the one-way link followed the module datasheet \citep{kineis2023kim1}, but commercial plans differ between providers and change over time.
  \item Loss: no measured loss rate was available for the service; therefore, loss probabilities and burstiness were swept. Field measurements could narrow the plan recommendation.
  \item Hardware: the study was trace-driven. The encoder has an exact algorithm in time linear in the number of readings \citep{imai1988}, a day of four parameters at 10-minute sampling occupies approximately 1.2\,kB, and a Reed--Solomon code over GF($2^8$) needs only table look-ups \citep{reed1960}; all of this suits a microcontroller, but neither was run on a node.
  \item Sensor error: the guarantee bounds the error added by the encoder, not the error of the sensor. A sensor that already uses part of the USGS error budget leaves less for the encoder; reserving half of the budget for the sensor raised the median need to \sensHalfMed{} bytes per day (Section~\ref{sec:sens}).
  \item Tag practice: the baseline modelled the pseudo-random resending of satellite-linked tags within one day. Real tags rotate a buffer over several days, which adds a form of repetition across days that was not modelled.
  \item Tolerances: the USGS rating defines accuracy relative to the measured record. Other agencies use other limits, and the guarantee refers to the sensor readings, not to the river itself; sensor errors are outside its scope.
  \item Low-cost deployments: both records had many gaps, which left \nDaysInws{} and \nDaysWeija{} complete days. Their results are indicative.
  \item Day boundaries: the reconstruction of a day starts from the previous day's last value. A lost anchor weakens the guarantee of the following day until a new value arrives.
\end{itemize}
