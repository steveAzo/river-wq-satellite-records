\begin{abstract}
Satellite Internet of Things services reach remote rivers but sell only a few short, one-way messages per day, and the server never learns which messages were lost. This study asked what a server can soundly decide about water-quality thresholds from the bytes that arrive, and spent the bytes on the decisions a receiver may need rather than on reconstruction. A node sends an error-bounded record within the accuracy of an excellent USGS record, plus witnesses of hard cases: quantised daily extrema and a conductance rate pair. A three-valued rule answers exceeded, not exceeded, or undetermined; the answers are proved correct for any pattern of lost messages under stated conditions on the data and the site profile. A first confirmation of an earlier version failed its integrity hypothesis and used an unfair comparator; the revised method was confirmed once on 35 sealed rivers (2022) at byte level. All three pre-registered tests held. On unconfigured thresholds, witnesses decided 0.182 to 0.226 more hard cases than a node sending configured decision flags plus the record, and 0.197 to 0.275 more than the record alone. The cost was 0.026 to 0.082 in whole-record recovery; at six messages per day under independent loss (0.082) it exceeded the design's own adoption limit of 0.08, and the design was adopted post hoc. No certain decision was wrong in the tested configurations. Flags remained better on the thresholds they encode. A 24-bit check guards against random corruption, not deliberate forgery. The evidence is trace-driven simulation with modelled loss.
\end{abstract}
