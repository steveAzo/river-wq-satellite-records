\begin{abstract}
Satellite Internet of Things services reach remote rivers but sell only a few short, one-way messages per day, and the server never learns which messages were lost. This study asked what a server can decide about regulatory thresholds from the bytes that arrive. A node sends an error-bounded record within the accuracy that the U.S. Geological Survey requires of an excellent record, plus witnesses of hard cases: quantised daily extrema and a conductance rate pair. A three-valued certificate rule answers exceeded, not exceeded, or undetermined, and is sound under stated assumptions. The method was developed on two years of data (38 and 31 rivers), revised after an earlier confirmation failed one hypothesis and an internal review found its comparator mis-modelled, and confirmed once on 35 sealed rivers (2022) at byte level, with each message protected by a 24-bit check. All three tests of the method held. On thresholds that nobody configured, targeted witnesses decided 0.18 to 0.23 more hard cases than a node sending configured decision flags plus the record, and 0.20 to 0.28 more than the record alone, at six or twelve messages per day with 30\% loss. The cost was 0.03 to 0.08 in whole-record recovery; the design was adopted although this cost exceeded its own written adoption limit, and the cost was tested instead. No certain decision was wrong, and 41,668 message blocks passed a byte-level round trip. Flags remained better on the thresholds they encode. The evidence is trace-driven; no satellite hardware was used.
\end{abstract}
