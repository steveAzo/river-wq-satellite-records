 A regulatory-grade river water-quality record in a few
                   satellite messages a day

                     Kofi Sarpong Adu-Manua,, S. K. Azongoa

           aDepartment of Computer Science, University of Ghana, Accra, Ghana

Abstract

Many polluted rivers, such as those affected by illegal gold mining in Ghana,
lie beyond terrestrial network coverage, and satellite Internet of Things ser-
vices that reach them sell a small number of short, one-way messages per day.
This study asked how much data a regulatory-grade water-quality record re-
quires, and how such a record can be delivered over these links. A record was
taken as guaranteed when every reconstructed value stayed within the United
States Geological Survey (USGS) accuracy rating of an excellent record. On
39 USGS river sites, the fewest values that guaranteed a day of temperature,
conductance, dissolved oxygen, and pH needed a median of 15 bytes, 1.8% of
the raw data, and at most 10 messages of 23 bytes. Because the satellite link
gives no acknowledgements, the design that protected this minimal record
with an erasure code across messages was compared with current practice in
a pre-registered study: designs were tuned on 2023 data and confirmed on
a sealed 2024 year. At six messages a day with 30% message loss, the pro-
posed design guaranteed 89% of site-days, against 34% for the same record
sent without protection and 43% for the scheme used by satellite-linked an-
imal tags; repetition of the record performed similarly to the code. With a
buffer, six messages a day kept 90% of days guaranteed within a week at 35
of 39 sites, and a 28-day pilot selected an adequate message plan for 85% of
sites. All seven pre-registered hypotheses held.

Keywords: water-quality monitoring, satellite Internet of Things, data
compression, erasure coding, error-bounded records, remote sensing
networks

1. Introduction

    Continuous water-quality monitoring underpins the regulation of rivers
that supply drinking water and receive pollution. Wireless sensor networks
have made it possible to measure temperature, specific conductance, dis-
solved oxygen, and pH every few minutes at low cost (Adu-Manu et al.,
2017). Deployments in Ghana and Kosovo have shown that such systems
work in practice, but they relied on cellular backhaul (Adu-Manu et al., 2020;
Ahmedi and Ahmedi, 2022). The rivers under the greatest pressure are of-
ten the furthest from that infrastructure. In Ghana, illegal small-scale gold
mining has degraded rivers in forested catchments where terrestrial coverage
is sparse (Duncan, 2020).

    Satellite Internet of Things (IoT) services now reach such sites with small,
low-power terminals (Centenaro et al., 2021). Their capacity, however, is sold
in messages, not bandwidth. A typical module transmits one-way messages
of about 23 bytes at 400 bit/s and receives no acknowledgement (Kinéis,
2023), and service plans offer a few to a few dozen messages per day. A four-
parameter sensor sampling every 10 minutes produces approximately 1,296
bytes per day, which would require tens of such messages. The question for
a monitoring agency is therefore not how to stream every reading, but what
a credible record of the river costs, and how to deliver that record when
messages are scarce and may be lost without notice.

    This study addressed that question with a definition of credibility taken
from regulatory practice. The USGS rates the accuracy of continuous water-
quality records, and an excellent record keeps temperature within 0.2 °C,
specific conductance within 3%, dissolved oxygen within 0.3 mg/L or 5%,
and pH within 0.2 units (Wagner et al., 2006). A day's record was called
guaranteed when every value reconstructed at the server stayed within these
limits. The study then asked four research questions:
1. RQ1: How many bytes per day does a guaranteed record require, and how

   does that need vary across sites, seasons, and parameters?
2. RQ2: Which satellite message plans can carry the record when a node

   buffers data across days, and with what delay?
3. RQ3: Which delivery design maximises the share of guaranteed days over

   a one-way link that loses an unknown share of messages, compared with
   the designs used in practice?
4. RQ4: Can an adequate message plan be chosen from a short pilot record?
    Each element of the answer has antecedents. Minimum-size piecewise-

linear approximations with a maximum-error bound are classical (Imai and
Iri, 1988) and have online versions for sensor streams (Soroush et al., 2008).
Compressing buffered sensor history to a bandwidth budget, including a
group leader that gives more bandwidth to the node with the largest er-

Erasure codes and unequal protection of compressed data are standard tools
(Reed and Solomon, 1960; Albanese et al., 1996). Satellite-linked animal tags
have long compressed ocean profiles into a few broken-stick points and re-
sent them pseudo-randomly over the one-way Argos system (Boehme et al.,
2009; Photopoulou et al., 2015). What has not been established is how
these elements perform together for regulatory-grade environmental records
on byte-priced, one-way satellite links, and what such a record costs on real
rivers. The contributions of this study are the following:
· A measurement of the cost of a USGS-excellent water-quality record on

   39 rivers over two years: a median of 15 bytes per day (1.8% of the raw
   data), with its variation across sites, months, and parameters.
· A delivery design for one-way links that sends the minimum guaranteed
   set of values protected by an erasure code across messages, together with
   a server-side certification of which parts of the record are guaranteed,
   obtained without any feedback.
· A pre-registered comparison with current practice, tuned on 2023 and
   confirmed on a sealed 2024 year and on two low-cost deployments, in
   which all seven hypotheses held.
· Design guidance for agencies: the message plan that suffices under inde-
   pendent and bursty loss, and a pilot-based rule that selects it.

and the designs. Section 4 describes the data and the pre-registered study.
Section 5 reports the results, Section 6 discusses their implications and limits,
and Section 7 concludes.

2. Related work

2.1. Error-bounded reduction of sensor records

    Sensor data reduction with guaranteed accuracy has two classical formu-
lations. The first fixes a precision and minimises communication: Olston
et al. (2003) adapted per-source filter widths so that continuous queries met
their precision constraints with the fewest updates, and Deligiannakis et al.
(2004) redistributed error thresholds within aggregation trees. The second

                                                3
fixes a budget and minimises error. Self-based regression (SBR) compresses
the buffered multi-signal history of a sensor node to a bandwidth budget
and supports a maximum-absolute-error objective; in its localised mode, a
group leader repeatedly gives residual bandwidth to the member with the
largest error (Deligiannakis et al., 2006). For a single signal, the fewest ver-
tices of a piecewise-linear approximation within a maximum error can be
found in linear time (Imai and Iri, 1988), also online (Soroush et al., 2008),
and many signals can be archived jointly under one maximum error (Gandhi
et al., 2009). Storage-side work has produced multi-resolution summaries for
drill-down queries (Ganesan et al., 2003), predictive archival with on-demand
retrieval (Desnoyers et al., 2005), and layered time-series encodings that de-
code at several error levels (Barbarioli et al., 2023). This study used the
minimum-size approximation as its node encoder. It did not propose a new
compression algorithm; it measured what that encoder costs for regulatory
tolerances on rivers, and how its output survives a link without acknowledge-
ments, which these works assume to be reliable.

2.2. Satellite IoT and one-way delivery

    Direct-to-satellite IoT has been studied mainly at the medium-access
layer: constellation design for revisit time (Capez et al., 2022), slotted ALOHA
with erasures at several satellites (Tondo et al., 2021), uplink transmission
policies (Alvarez et al., 2022), scheduling of LoRa uplinks to passing satel-
lites (Afhamisis and Palattella, 2022), and message replication for reliability
(Rathi and Borkotoky, 2025). These works treat packets as interchangeable
and do not address what the packets should contain. Terrestrial schemes for
bulk transfer of buffered data aim to deliver every byte (Abdelfadeel et al.,
2020). The closest practice to this study comes from animal telemetry. Con-
ductivity­temperature­depth satellite relay data loggers abstract each ocean
profile to a fixed number of points, store the resulting messages in a buffer,
and send them pseudo-randomly through the one-way Argos system so that
losses are spread over an unbiased sample (Boehme et al., 2009; Photopoulou
et al., 2015). That practice fixes the number of points, not the accuracy, and
it was included in this study as a baseline.

the current state of a source (Yates et al., 2021), including over random-access
and satellite links (Chen et al., 2021; Talli et al., 2026; Ramachandran and

3. System model

    Fig. 1 summarises the system. A sensor node at a remote river site sam-
ples several water-quality parameters, stores them, and once a day encodes
the day's record into a small number of satellite messages. The link delivers
each message or loses it, and the node never learns which. The server recon-
structs the record from the values that arrive and states which parts of it are
guaranteed.

3.1. Record and tolerance

    For parameter j of a site, let xj(t), t = 1, . . . , n, be the day's readings
and xj(0) the last reading of the previous day, which the server is assumed
to hold. The server reconstructs x^j(t) by linear interpolation between the
values it received, starting from xj(0), and holds the last received value to
the end of the day. The tolerance of each reading follows the USGS rating
of an excellent record (Wagner et al., 2006): 0.2 °C for temperature, 3% of
the value for specific conductance, the greater of 0.3 mg/L and 5% of the

3.3. Messages and link

    Each message carries 23 bytes (Kinéis, 2023): a one-byte header with a
three-bit day index and a five-bit sequence number, followed by seven values
of three bytes each. A value uses two bits for the parameter, 14 bits for the
reading (a resolution far finer than any tolerance in Section 3), and one byte
for its time slot. A plan allows Q messages per day. Plans of 1, 6, 12, 24,
and 96 messages per day were evaluated as illustrative tiers of commercial
offers.

    The link is one-way. Two loss models were used, because no measured loss
rate was available for the target service: independent loss with probability p,
and a two-state Gilbert channel (Gilbert, 1960; Elliott, 1963) that loses every
message in its bad state, with mean burst length three messages and the same
mean loss p. The values p  {0.1, 0.3, 0.5} span the erasure probabilities used
in direct-to-satellite studies (Tondo et al., 2021).

3.5. Decoding and certification

    The server decodes what arrived and reconstructs each parameter as de-
scribed above. Because sequence numbers identify which planned values
arrived, the server can certify, without feedback, every span between two
consecutive planned values that both arrived: the encoder guaranteed every
reading inside it within  times its tolerance. The certified share is the share
of readings inside such spans.

3.6. Buffering across days

    The need of a day varies (Section 5), so a node can carry the guaranteed
sets of demanding days into the spare capacity of quiet days. In the buffered
model, each day's guaranteed set joins a first-in, first-out backlog at the end
of the day. Every day the node sends one coded block of K data and Q - K
parity messages, filled from the oldest backlog first. A day is guaranteed if
every block that carried its values decoded, and its delay is the number of

                         7
days until its last block was sent. Because the link is one-way, a lost block
is not resent.

4. Data and study design

4.1. Data
    The main data were approved instantaneous values from 39 USGS river

sites in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Illinois, and Oregon (U.S. Geological Sur-
vey, 2016), recorded every 15 minutes at 28 sites, every 30 minutes at 9 sites,
and every 5 minutes and every hour at one site each. Dissolved oxygen was
used in mg/L, the unit of the accuracy rating. A site-day was used when
every gap within it spanned at most two readings; such gaps were bridged
linearly. The year 2023 (10,978 site-days) served for development and 2024
(12,221 site-days, 10,164 with all four parameters) for confirmation. Two low-
cost deployments were added: node 1 of the InWaterSense network on the
Sitnica River, Kosovo (Ahmedi and Ahmedi, 2022; Ahmedi, 2021), and the
Weija intake deployment in Ghana (Adu-Manu et al., 2020). InWaterSense
reports dissolved oxygen in percent saturation, which was converted to mg/L
with the freshwater solubility of Benson and Krause (1984) at the measured
temperature. Readings flagged as implausible by earlier cleaning were re-
moved. For Weija, only temperature and conductivity were used, because its
pH record was not physically plausible. Both low-cost records contain many
gaps, which left 22 and 49 complete site-days.

4.2. Pre-registered study
    The study followed a written plan. All design choices were made on the

2023 data: the repetition count r, the code dimension K, and the share of
messages used by tag practice were tuned for each plan, loss model, and loss
probability on half of a stratified sample of 780 site-days, and reported on the
other half. The plan, the tuned parameters, and the code were then frozen
and checksummed, and the hypotheses in Table 2 were fixed. Only then were
the 2024 data opened, once, after their checksums had been verified; the low-
cost deployments had not been used for any choice either. The confirmatory
runs applied the frozen parameters without change.

5.1. Cost of a guaranteed record (RQ1)

    Fig. 2 illustrates the encoder on a summer day of dissolved oxygen. Six
values chosen by the encoder kept the whole day within tolerance, whereas
six evenly spaced readings missed the steep afternoon rise and exceeded the
tolerance by a factor of more than two.

    Across all site-days, the guaranteed record was small (Fig. 3). Its median
size was 15 bytes per day, 1.8% of the raw data; the 90th percentile was 45
bytes, the 99th percentile 108 bytes, and the largest day needed 201 bytes,
or 10 messages. The record fitted into a single 23-byte message on 63% of
site-days. The development year gave almost the same distribution (median
18 bytes, 99th percentile 111 bytes).

    The need varied with the site and the season. Site medians ranged from
3 to 57 bytes per day, and within a site the 99th percentile was typically 3.3
times the median. The median need rose from 6 bytes per day in January to
24 bytes per day in June (Fig. 4); the highest needs occurred from June to
August. At sites that measured all four parameters, temperature accounted
for 41% of the values in the guaranteed records, dissolved oxygen for 26%,
specific conductance for 23%, and pH for 10%. Temperature, often treated
as a slowly varying baseline, was therefore the most expensive parameter to
keep within its tight tolerance.

5.3. Buffering across days (RQ2)
    Fig. 6 shows the share of sites that met the buffered target. Under in-

dependent loss with p = 0.3, six messages per day met it at 35 of 39 sites
(hypothesis H2), with guaranteed days typically delivered on the following
day (median delay 1 day in development). Under bursty loss, six messages
per day met the target at only 17 sites, because one burst can erase a whole
day's block; 12 messages per day restored it at 35 sites (H2b). One message
per day met the target at no site.

6. Discussion

6.1. Guidance for monitoring agencies
    The results translate into five recommendations for remote river monitor-

ing over satellite IoT:
1. Send the guaranteed record, not the readings. A day within the USGS

   excellent-record tolerance needed a median of 15 bytes, which is approx-
   imately one fiftieth of the raw data. Evenly spaced readings at the same
   size violated the tolerance on most days at every loss probability tested
   (Table 3).
2. Protect the record against loss. The design that is optimal on a reliable
   link guaranteed only 34% of days at a loss probability of 0.3, and more
   capacity did not repair it. At that loss probability, repetition and an
   erasure code both raised the guaranteed share above 80%. The code is
   preferable where records are larger, as at the low-cost sites, and repetition
   is a simpler choice where they are small.

6.2. Relation to earlier work

    The encoder and the code are established techniques (Imai and Iri, 1988;
Soroush et al., 2008; Reed and Solomon, 1960), and budgeted compression
of sensor history, including allocation of bandwidth towards the node with
the largest error, was proposed for multi-hop sensor networks (Deligiannakis
et al., 2006). The findings of this study qualify that line of work for one-way
links: the design that is best when every message arrives was the worst of
the guaranteed-record designs once messages could be lost. They also qualify
current satellite-tag practice (Boehme et al., 2009; Photopoulou et al., 2015),
which fixes the number of points rather than the accuracy; by construction,
such a scheme spends capacity on quiet days and cannot guarantee the toler-
ance on active ones. Freshness-oriented scheduling (Yates et al., 2021; Talli

                                                            15
et al., 2026) addresses a different objective, the latest value, which a guaran-
teed record covers as a special case.

6.3. Limitations
    The results carry the following limitations:

· Message plans: the tiers of 1 to 96 messages per day were illustrative; the
   message size and the one-way link followed the module datasheet (Kinéis,
   2023), but commercial plans differ between providers and change over
   time.

· Loss: no measured loss rate was available for the service, so loss proba-
   bilities and burstiness were swept. Field measurements could narrow the
   plan recommendation.

· Hardware: the study was trace-driven. The encoder and the code are
   light enough for a microcontroller (Imai and Iri, 1988; Reed and Solomon,
   1960), but they were not run on a node.

· Tolerances: the USGS rating defines accuracy relative to the measured
   record. Other agencies use other limits, and the guarantee refers to the
   sensor readings, not to the river itself; sensor errors are outside its scope.

· Low-cost deployments: both records had many gaps, which left 22 and 49
   complete days. Their results are indicative.

· Day boundaries: the reconstruction of a day starts from the previous day's
   last value. A lost anchor weakens the guarantee of the following day until
   a new value arrives.

7. Conclusions

    This study measured what a regulatory-grade water-quality record costs
and how it can be delivered over the one-way, message-limited satellite links
that reach remote rivers. On 39 USGS sites over two years, a record within
the USGS excellent-record tolerance needed a median of 15 bytes per day and
at most 10 messages of 23 bytes. Delivered without protection, such a record
was guaranteed on only 34% of days at a message loss of 30%. Protected
by an erasure code or by repetition, it was guaranteed on more than 80% of
days at the same loss with six messages per day, and on most sites within a
week when a node buffered its data. A 28-day pilot was sufficient to choose
the plan at 85% of sites. All seven pre-registered hypotheses held on a sealed
confirmation year.

                                               16
    For agencies monitoring rivers such as those affected by illegal mining in
Ghana, the result means that a credible continuous record does not require
a high-capacity link. It requires the right bytes, protected against loss, on
a plan sized from local data. Future work should measure message loss on
operational satellite services, run the encoder on field hardware, and extend
the guarantee to the event alarms that operators need in real time.
