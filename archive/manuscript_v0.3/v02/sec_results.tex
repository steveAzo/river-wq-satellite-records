\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

All seven pre-registered hypotheses held on the 2024 confirmation data (Table~\ref{tab:hyp}). This section reports the results by research question; unless stated otherwise, numbers refer to the 2024 data.

\subsection{Cost of a guaranteed record (RQ1)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:example} illustrates the encoder on a summer day of dissolved oxygen. Six values chosen by the encoder kept the whole day within tolerance, whereas six evenly spaced readings missed the steep afternoon rise and exceeded the tolerance by a factor of more than two.

\caption{Dissolved oxygen on one day of 2023 at a development site. (a) The fewest values that keep every reading within tolerance. (b) The same number of evenly spaced readings. The shaded band is the USGS excellent-record tolerance.}
\label{fig:example}
\end{figure}

Across all site-days, the guaranteed record was small (Fig.~\ref{fig:need}). Its median size was \needMedConf{} bytes per day (95\% interval \ciNeed{} bytes), \needShareConf{} of the raw readings; the 90th percentile was \needPninetyConf{} bytes, the 99th percentile \needPnnConf{} bytes, and the largest day needed \needMaxConf{} bytes, or \needMsgMaxConf{} messages. The record fitted into a single 23-byte message on \needOneMsgConf{} of site-days. The development year gave almost the same distribution (median \needMedDev{} bytes, 99th percentile \needPnnDev{} bytes).

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figs/need_ecdf.pdf}
\caption{Cumulative distribution of the bytes per day needed for a guaranteed record. Vertical lines mark the value capacity of 1, 6, and 12 messages per day. Days that needed no value are drawn at 1 byte.}
\label{fig:need}
\end{figure}

The need varied with the site and the season. Site medians ranged from \siteMedMin{} to \siteMedMax{} bytes per day, and within a site the 99th percentile was typically \siteTailRatio{} times the median. The median need rose from \monthMin{} bytes per day in \monthMinName{} to \monthMax{} bytes per day in \monthMaxName{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:month}); the highest needs occurred from June to August. At sites that measured all four parameters, temperature accounted for \parShareTemp{} of the values in the guaranteed records, dissolved oxygen for \parShareDox{}, specific conductance for \parShareSc{}, and pH for \parSharePh{}. Temperature, often treated as a slowly varying baseline, was therefore the most expensive parameter to keep within its tight tolerance.

The low-cost deployments were more expensive. InWaterSense needed a median of \needInws{} bytes per day, \needInwsRatio{} times the USGS median, which confirmed hypothesis H7. The cause was not isolated in this study; candidate causes include sensor noise and fouling, and the few complete days available. Weija, with two parameters, needed \needWeija{} bytes per day.

\subsection{Delivery over a one-way link (RQ3)}
Table~\ref{tab:delivery} and Fig.~\ref{fig:designs} compare the designs. At six messages per day and an independent loss probability of 0.3, the erasure-code design guaranteed \gCodeIndPct{} of site-days (95\% interval \ciCodeInd{}). The reliable-link design, which sends the tighter record without protection, guaranteed \gRelIndPct{} (\ciRelInd{}); tag practice guaranteed \gSrdlIndPct{} (\ciSrdlInd{}); and evenly spaced readings guaranteed \gRawIndPct{} (\ciRawInd{}). By median over site-days, the code reached \hThreeRatio{} times the guaranteed share of the reliable-link design (hypothesis H3; 95\% interval \ciHThree{}) and exceeded tag practice by \hFourDiff{} (H4). The code was better than the reliable-link design on \pairBeatRel{} of site-days and better than tag practice on \pairBeatSrdl{}. The development data had given \gCodeIndDev{} and \gRelIndDev{} for the code and the reliable-link design; thus, the gap carried over to the new year.

Repetition of the guaranteed record performed close to the code. Its mean was \gRepIndPct{} against \gCodeIndPct{} under independent loss, a small but consistent difference (\diffCodeRep{}, 95\% interval \ciDiffCodeRep{}), and equal under bursty loss (\gRepBurPct{} and \gCodeBurPct{}); its median was slightly higher than that of the code. Both exceeded the reliable-link design by at least \hFiveMin{} in every setting of six, 12, and 24 messages per day with loss probabilities of 0.3 and 0.5 (H5). The difference between them appeared on the low-cost records, whose larger guaranteed sets left less room for copies: there, the code guaranteed \gCodeLCPct{} of site-days and repetition \gRepLCPct{}.

Two further results bound these conclusions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Capacity alone did not help: at 24 messages per day and $p = 0.3$, the reliable-link design still guaranteed only \gRelTwentyFour{} of site-days, because a single lost message removes values from a record that has no redundancy.
  \item One message per day was too few: no design reached more than \gCodeOneMax{}, since a single message cannot carry both a record and protection.
\end{itemize} The server could certify \certCode{} of readings with the code, against \certRel{} with the reliable-link design.

\subsection{Buffering across days (RQ2)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pooling} shows the share of sites that met the buffered target. Under independent loss with $p = 0.3$, six messages per day met it at \poolSixInd{} of \nSites{} sites (hypothesis H2), with guaranteed days typically delivered on the following day (median delay \poolDelayMed{} day in development). Under bursty loss, six messages per day met the target at only \poolSixBur{} sites, because one burst can erase a whole day's block; 12 messages per day restored it at \poolTwelveBur{} sites (H2b). One message per day met the target at \poolOnePhrase{}.

\subsection{Choosing a plan from a pilot (RQ4)}
The plan chosen from the first 28 days of 2024 was sufficient for the rest of the year at \sizeSuff{} of sites, and it was never more than one tier larger than necessary (\sizeOver{} of sites; hypothesis H6). At \sizeShortLow{} of the \sizeShort{} sites whose pilot underestimated the need, the median need during the pilot was lower than over the rest of the year, which is consistent with the seasonal rise in Fig.~\ref{fig:month}.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{sec:sens}
Table~\ref{tab:sens} summarises the exploratory sensitivity analyses of the daily need. The results above used exact readings; an encoder that targets 0.975 times the tolerance, which keeps the transmitted 13-bit values within it too, needed a median of \sensQMed{} bytes per day and \sensQPct{} more values in total, which is less than one message on the most demanding days. Reserving half of the error budget for the sensor raised the median need to \sensHalfMed{} bytes, \sensHalfRatio{} times the main result, whereas the USGS rating of a good record needed only \sensGoodMed{} bytes. The two simpler encoders were more expensive: greedy online segmentation needed \sensGreedyPct{} more values than the fewest-values encoder, and deadband reporting \sensDeadRatio{} times as many. Coarser sampling did not reduce the need: at the sites sampled every 15 minutes, the median was \sensFifteenMed{} bytes per day, and it stayed at \sensXTwoMed{} and \sensXFourMed{} bytes when only every second or fourth reading was kept, because the need reflects the variation of the river rather than the number of readings.
